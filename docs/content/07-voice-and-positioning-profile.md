Future prompts (see `03-career-output-prompt-pack.md`) should read this alongside Files 01/02 so that generated copy sounds like you, not like a generic resume generator — while every keyword in Section 4 below stays verbatim regardless of how the surrounding sentence is written.

**A rule this file enforces on anything written from it:** nothing in Section 5 (raw material) should ever appear verbatim in outward-facing copy. ## 1. How you actually work

You read existing docs first, take notes, and talk to people about a problem before building — and only reach for a prototype/PoC when it's genuinely needed. Under real deadline pressure, the one thing you won't compromise on is testing: you always test locally with your own judgment (happy paths and edge cases) even when a QA suite already exists. In the AI era specifically, you've deliberately shifted from an execution-first instinct toward research-and-planning-first, because with execution increasingly automated, the specification and planning stage is where the real engineering judgment now lives — this is a good, current, and honestly-earned observation to use directly in a "how AI has changed my work" narrative.

When something breaks in production, you talk to the person who reported it first, then investigate logs, then reproduce locally if needed — people before logs, deliberately.

## 2. Values

You get frustrated by systems built on unrecorded, inconsistent assumptions — decisions made once and never written down, never followed consistently elsewhere in the codebase. The flip side is a real, evidenced value: standards and patterns should be followed and documented. (Worth knowing: this is exactly the discipline ClearEdgeIQ's ADR-style decision log and documentation-hygiene passes already demonstrate — your stated value and your actual work are already aligned, which is a strong, authentic thread to pull on in interviews or a LinkedIn About section.)

You feel genuinely proud of your work when a client tells you that you understood their point of view — that you thought about it from the user's side and caught edge cases they hadn't articulated — and when you see their vision fully working, not just technically complete.

Why this field, in your own words, cleaned up slightly for professional use: you're drawn to what's underneath the interface — how a system actually behaves under real stress and real use, not just how it looks. UI is visible and easy to admire; the harder, more interesting problem is what happens when things are pushed.

## 3. How you communicate

With non-technical stakeholders: analogy and plain language, aimed at genuine understanding, not at demonstrating technical depth. With semi-technical audiences: a step-by-step walkthrough. You adapt to the person in front of you rather than using one fixed style.

When a client or stakeholder pushes back on a technical decision, your real pattern (and a genuinely strong one for Solution Architect / Forward Deployed Engineer positioning): you don't agree on the spot, even when you're confident you're right. You take the concern seriously, ask for time, go research it properly, and look for a middle ground that doesn't compromise the technical integrity of the system and respects the client's budget. When the stakes are real, you write a concise report laying out the pros, cons, and likely ripple effects before recommending a path. This is a complete, usable "how do you handle pushback" interview answer as-is.

Feedback you've actually received and that's worth using directly: colleagues have told you that you explain problems and solutions thoroughly — sometimes almost too thoroughly — which is a real signal of communication strength worth stating plainly rather than downplaying.
